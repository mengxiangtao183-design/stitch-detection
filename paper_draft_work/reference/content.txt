[Normal] EPSPM: Efficient and Precise Stitch Pitch Measurement Framework via Deblurring and Segmentation
[Heading 1] Abstract
[Normal] Current industrial sewing production lines primarily rely on manual visual inspection for stitch quality assessment, which introduces efficiency bottlenecks. This task becomes particularly challenging when the stitch and fabric are of the same color. Motion blur from high-speed sewing, complex background noise, and the subtle nature of stitch pitch features further complicate automated detection. To address these issues, this study proposes the Efficient and Precise Stitch Pitch Measurement (EPSPM) framework, which integrates the Efficient Stitch Deblurring Network (ESDNet), the lightweight Precise Stitch Segmentation network (PSSNet), and the Stitch Pitch Measurement Module (SPMM). Through multi-module optimization, EPSPM enhances the processing of low-contrast stitch patterns. Experimental results on the self-constructed StitchPitchDataset (SPD) demonstrate that both ESDNet and PSSNet achieve the best performance across two evaluation metrics within their respective tasks, with SPMM achieving an error below 0.20 mm for low-contrast stitches and 0.15 mm for high-contrast stitches, processing individual images in under 0.035 seconds, meeting real-world production requirements. Furthermore, the stitch pitch-based approach enables effective identification of stitch defect types, providing a reliable solution for defect detection even in same-color scenarios, thereby supporting robust stitch quality assessment in the garment industry.

[Normal] Stitch pitch measurement; Stitch defect detection; Algorithm framework; Motion deblurring; Stitch segmentation
[Heading 1] 1 Introduction
[Normal] In the era of Industry 4.0, intelligent manufacturing and automation are pivotal in driving the transformation of the garment industry. However, challenges in product quality and detection efficiency remain significant barriers to progress [1] . The uniformity and consistency of stitch pitch, critical to product appearance and quality [2] , are particularly vital in real-world production environments. Currently, industrial sewing lines rely heavily on manual inspection for stitch quality assessment [3] , which is susceptible to subjective bias and fails to meet stringent process requirements. Addressing these challenges necessitates an efficient and intelligent method for detecting whether stitch pitch adheres to process standards, with computer vision and deep learning technologies offering viable solutions for the garment industry [4] .
[Normal] Computer vision applications in garment production have primarily focused on fabric surface defect detection. For instance, Guo et al. summarized learning-based approaches for intelligent fabric defect detection from both machine learning and deep learning perspectives [5] , while Liu et al. proposed an enhanced YOLOv4 algorithm to improve the accuracy and robustness of fabric defect detection [6] . Despite these advancements, existing research has paid relatively little attention to sewing stitch defects, such as stitch breaks or stitch irregularities [7-11]. Recent progress in deep learning for image processing has bolstered the ability to extract complex semantic features, laying a technical foundation for addressing sewing stitch defect detection[12,13]. Kim et al. [14] developed a method for detecting stitch breaks using VGG-16 feature maps, eliminating the need for manual feature engineering. Ma et al. [15] introduced an improved YOLOv4-Tiny model for sewing stitch quality detection, incorporating a SoftPool-optimized convolutional attention mechanism and a Soft-SPPF module to enhance stitch point feature extraction. Jung et al. [16] constructed the StitchingNet dataset and proposed a deep transfer learning approach for sewing defect detection in garment manufacturing. Hu et al. [17] presented a U-Net-based method for detecting stitch breaks in fabric sewing, using a trained network to extract stitch features and a template matching algorithm to derive feature contours, building a model for break detection and anomaly tracing based on distances between adjacent contours. While these methods demonstrate capability in detecting stitch defects, they face limitations in real industrial scenarios: motion blur from high-speed equipment, color similarity between fabric and stitch complicating defect identification, and increased complexity and risk of misjudgment. Additionally, detection must balance real-time performance with high precision to ensure effective control.
[Normal] Motion blur degrades the clarity of fine stitch features, and deep learning-based deblurring methods offer a promising solution. Tao et al. [18] and Cho et al. [19] employed pyramid structures and single encoder-decoder designs, respectively, to optimize the restoration of non-uniformly blurred images, with the former focusing on resolution recovery and the latter reducing computational complexity while maintaining deblurring performance. Kupyn et al. [20] improved deblurring efficiency in complex blur scenarios by integrating generative adversarial networks with multi-scale feature fusion, while Lu et al. [21] proposed MRDNet, an effective CNN-based segmentation network for surface defect detection. Zamir et al. [22] and Chen et al. [23] simplified network designs by removing nonlinear activation functions (e.g., ReLU, Sigmoid), demonstrating superior performance in image restoration tasks like deblurring while reducing computational costs. Mao et al. [24] combined Fourier transforms with deep residual learning to enhance deblurring outcomes. Tsai et al. [25] introduced a blur-aware attention mechanism to focus on blurred regions in dynamic scenes, achieving efficient and precise deblurring. To address the impact of motion blur on stitch clarity in fabric defect detection, our previously proposed DeblurGAN-BSV3 [26] integrated asymmetric feature fusion and image sharpness evaluation modules within a feature pyramid network to optimize deblurring performance. However, existing deblurring methods struggle with recovering edge details of fine stitch features and adapting to dynamic scenes, falling short of meeting the core requirements for stitch clarity in measurement.
[Normal] Sewing stitch segments share similarities with biological and medical image segmentation due to their fine features and regular distribution, offering valuable insights for addressing color similarity challenges in stitch pitch detection. U-Net [27] and its variant UNet++ [28] established the foundation for image segmentation through encoder-decoder structures and skip connections to fuse multi-scale features for efficient pixel-level segmentation, though their performance is limited in low-contrast images and noisy environments. The rise of Transformers in computer vision has led to models like ConvNeXt [29] , which capture long-range dependencies via self-attention to improve segmentation in complex backgrounds. However, their high parameter counts and computational complexity prompted subsequent studies, such as UneXt [30] , ACC-UNet [31] , EGE-UNet [32] , DeformableLKA [33] , and Rolling-UNet [34] , to explore combinations of convolutions and MLPs, Transformer-CNN hybrids, and deformable attention with multi-directional feature fusion. These efforts resulted in lightweight networks, solutions optimized for resource-constrained scenarios, and dynamic feature extraction methods, reflecting a trend from traditional convolutions to hybrid architectures. Dinh et al. [35] developed U-Lite, achieving superior performance with reduced parameters, balancing efficiency and practicality. Nonetheless, when applied to sewing stitch segmentation, these methods still face challenges in maintaining both high precision and real-time processing under low-contrast and noisy industrial conditions.
[Normal] To address these challenges, we constructed the StitchPitchDataset (SPD) for stitch pitch measurement and developed the Efficient and Precise Stitch Pitch Measurement (EPSPM) framework. This framework comprises three core components: the Efficient Stitch Deblurring Network (ESDNet), the Precise Stitch Segmentation network (PSSNet), and the Stitch Pitch Measurement Module (SPMM). Building upon recent advances in efficient convolutional and attention-based architectures [36-39], EPSPM integrates task-specific modules to enhance both accuracy and efficiency. In ESDNet, a tailored temporal feature extraction strategy and a Multi-Scale Attention Aggregation (MSAA) module are introduced for rapid and adaptive stitch image deblurring. PSSNet employs a Gradient Aggregation (GA) module in its initial layer to strengthen shallow feature representation, and a Lightweight Dilated Bottleneck (LDB) module between the encoder and decoder to improve local feature robustness and diversity for high-precision segmentation. Finally, the segmentation output is fed into SPMM, which performs adaptive denoising, stitch point localization, and coherence analysis to accurately measure stitch pitch and assess sewing defects based on stitch pitch variations, achieving robust performance on SPD. Collectively, EPSPM provides an efficient and reliable solution for automated defect detection in the garment industry. The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows:
[List Paragraph] Construction of the SPD: SPD is designed specifically for stitch pitch-based sewing defect detection, comprising 2,800 deblurring image pairs, 1,870 segmentation image pairs, and 208 stitch pitch measurement images.
[List Paragraph] Design of the ESDNet Deblurring Network: ESDNet incorporates the IncepFFT Block, which integrates frequency-domain and multi-scale convolutions to extract image features for rapid deblurring.
[List Paragraph] Design of the PSSNet for low-contrast scenarios: PSSNet combines customized illumination strategies with a lightweight architecture to enhance segmentation precision under challenging color-similarity conditions.
[List Paragraph] Design of the SPMM: SPMM employs adaptive denoising, geometric stitch point localization, and coherence analysis to achieve stitch pitch measurement with ±0.15 mm accuracy for high-contrast stitches and ±0.20 mm for low-contrast stitches. The complete EPSPM pipeline requires 0.035s per image, enabling defect detection based on stitch pitch analysis.
[Normal] The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 discusses the design of deblurring and segmentation algorithms; Section 3 describes the SPMM; Section 4 outlines the experiments; Section 5 presents the experimental results and analysis; and Section 6 provides the conclusions.
[Heading 1] 2 Methodology
[Heading 2] 2.1 Overall algorithm workflow
[Normal] The overall workflow of the proposed algorithm for stitch pitch measurement is illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, a camera captures images of the industrial sewing process. These images then undergo quality assessment via a composite scoring method—integrating Laplacian variance and structural similarity—to determine blur level. Images scoring below 0.5 are classified as blurred, and those above as sharp. Blurred images are processed by ESDNet, which employs the IncepFFT module to integrate frequency-domain and multi-scale features, restoring clear stitch patterns. Sharp images bypass this step and proceed directly to the next stage. Subsequently, deblurred or sharp images are fed into a data augmentation module. This module applies random stretching and rotation operations to enhance data diversity. The images are then processed by PSSNet—optimized with customized illumination—to segment stitches.
[Normal] Fig. 1. Overall algorithm workflow.
[Normal] Based on the segmentation results, scenes are classified as low-contrast or high-contrast depending on the color contrast between stitches and fabrics. In low-contrast scenarios, an adaptive denoising module is activated to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. Finally, the processed segmentation results are input into the SPMM, which uses geometric localization and coherence analysis to accurately measure stitch pitch and assess compliance with process requirements.
[Heading 2] 2.2 Image blur assessment method
[Normal] During the measurement process, an industrial camera continuously captures both sharp and blurred images. Deblurring all images, regardless of quality, would increase inference time. Sharp images do not adversely affect subsequent segmentation tasks, and subjecting them to deblurring may degrade image quality, prolong detection time, and lead to resource inefficiency. [Heading 2] 2.3 Deblurring algorithm
[Heading 3] 2.3.1 Architecture design of ESDNet
[Normal] The proposed image restoration framework ESDNet is shown in Fig. 4. Designed as a multi-scale feature fusion deep network, ESDNet aims to remove motion blur from industrial sewing stitch images and restore clear stitch structures. The network consists of three primary components: feature extraction, feature decoding, and multi-scale attention fusion. Although InceptionNeXt effectively captures features through multi-branch convolutions, it predominantly relies on temporal domain information. This dependence limits its ability to process subtle stitch features, particularly in low-contrast or motion-blurred scenarios where spatial details are difficult to recover. To overcome these limitations, we optimize the network architecture by incorporating a frequency-domain and multi-scale feature fusion mechanism, enhancing the perception of subtle stitch features and improving adaptability to complex environments, as shown in Table 1.
[Normal] The Stem layer converts input images into feature representations suitable for deep processing. Subsequently, four MetaNeXtStage layers extract multi-scale features with channel dimensions of 32, 96, 128, and 256, capturing stitch information from low-level textures to high-level semantics. We further propose the IncepFFT Block (see Section 2.3.2) as a token mixer, which combines spatial- and frequency-domain processing to enhance blurred stitch edge representations while maintaining low computational complexity for real-time industrial applications.
[Normal] The multi-scale attention fusion component employs the MSAA module, which utilizes FusionConv to integrate multi-scale features, upsampling them to resolutions of 256×256, 128×128, and 64×64. Compared with conventional feature concatenation strategies, MSAA uses 3×3, 5×5, and 7×7 convolutional kernels combined with spatial and channel attention to strengthen low-contrast stitch feature expression, suppress background noise, and optimize feature selection through attention-weighted processing.
[Normal] The feature decoding stage replaces the traditional layer-by-layer upsampling strategy with bilinear interpolation combined with frequency-domain residual blocks, achieving more efficient feature reconstruction. Specifically, based on the frequency-domain residual module proposed in [24] , this design enhances high-frequency details within multi-scale fused features, thereby improving edge clarity. Extracted features undergo 2×, 4×, 8×, and 16× bilinear upsampling to rapidly restore geometric structures and stitch contours, leveraging low computational complexity. Depth-separable Overparameterized Convolution (Do-conv) unifies channel counts to 32, concatenating initial, top-layer, and the three multi-scale fused representations. The integrated features are then reconstructed into a three-channel deblurred output image. Owing to the regularity of stitch patterns, this design minimizes redundant computation, effectively restores blurred images, and satisfies real-time industrial requirements.

[Heading 2] 2.4 Segmentation Algorithm
[Normal] PSSNet, a stitch segmentation framework illustrated in Fig. 6. PSSNet adopts the classic U-Net architecture, comprising an encoder, decoder, bottleneck layer, and skip connections. The encoder and decoder each consist of five downsampling and upsampling stages, respectively. Each encoder and decoder layer integrates an improved Res DA-Conv module to enhance feature extraction. Prior to Res DA-Conv processing, the GA module optimizes initial feature representation. The bottleneck layer employs multi-scale Res DA-Conv with residual connections and different dilation rates of 1, 2, and 3 to capture multi-scale contextual information, improving modeling of complex stitch structures. The LDB module between the encoder and decoder enhances local feature robustness and diversity, ensuring decoded features fully utilize initial encoding information. Unlike cell and organ segmentation tasks, which involve fewer scenarios with uniform shapes and low contrast, stitch segmentation requires addressing diverse stitch patterns and low-contrast challenges in complex environments. PSSNet enhances multi-directional feature extraction while maintaining acceptable inference speed, improving segmentation accuracy for fine and complex stitches.
[Heading 3] 2.4.1 GA and LDB
[Normal] Addressing the specific demands of stitch segmentation, balancing accuracy and speed is a critical challenge. To this end, PSSNet incorporates the GA and LDB modules to enhance network performance. The GA module, based on gradient aggregation, employs a multi-directional feature extraction layer using axial depthwise convolutions (e.g., 7×1 and 1×7 kernels) to capture gradient information in horizontal, vertical, and diagonal directions. Feature fusion efficiency is further enhanced through residual connections and weighted fusion layers. The LDB module employs a lightweight dilated bottleneck structure, including an input dimensionality reduction layer using 1×1 convolution to halve input feature channels, a dilated depthwise convolution layer employing 3×1 and 1×3 kernels with varying dilation rates to expand the receptive field, and a residual connection layer that effectively integrates input and output features to mitigate gradient vanishing and enhance stability. The GA module improves adaptability to complex gradient distributions through aggregation, while the LDB module enables efficient feature extraction via its lightweight dilated bottleneck.

[Heading 3] 2.4.2 Res DA-Conv
[Normal] Due to the complex characteristics of stitch images, including multi-directional patterns, high noise levels, and low contrast, the Vision Permutator [41] , which encodes features through linear projections along only height and width dimensions, has limitations. Similarly, while the axial convolution used in U-Lite offers low parameter counts, its limited coverage struggles to address stitch segmentation in complex backgrounds. The Res DA-Conv module with a snowflake-shaped receptive field enhances multi-scale feature extraction by incorporating 45° and 135° directional sampling alongside axial convolutions. [Heading 2] 3.1 Denoising Module
[Normal] After processing through the PSSNet, the resulting binary segmentation images are used as the initial inputs for stitch pitch measurement. For high-contrast stitches, the binary images clearly preserve the stitch structures and can be directly used to construct the stitch point set. However, for low-contrast stitches (e.g., white stitches on white fabric), factors such as fabric wrinkles and lighting variations may introduce dense or large noise points in the binary image. Therefore, morphological opening is applied to low-contrast stitch images to smooth the binary image through a combination of erosion and dilation. Specifically, the segmentation output generated by PSSNet is first binarized using a fixed threshold to enhance the distinction between stitch regions and the background. Subsequently, morphological opening operations with rectangular structuring elements of varying sizes are applied in two iterations, each tailored to different noise conditions, as illustrated in Fig. 9. [Heading 2] 3.2 Stitch point localization and pitch calculation
[Normal] The objective of stitch point localization is to accurately extract the centroid coordinates of individual stitch points from the binary image and construct an ordered stitch point set for subsequent stitch pitch calculation. High-contrast stitch images are directly processed for localization, whereas low-contrast images utilize the binarized and denoised outputs produced by the preprocessing stage described in Section 3.1. This differentiated strategy enhances computational efficiency while preserving the geometric integrity of stitch features. The localization process is accomplished via connected component analysis and region property extraction. Specifically, the algorithm first applies a connected component labeling procedure to the input binary image, assigning a unique integer label to each connected region to identify potential stitch areas. Subsequently, region property analysis computes the centroid coordinates and area of each connected region. For each valid region, a stitch point object containing the centroid coordinates is generated and stored within a stitch point set, which serves as a structured collection for subsequent geometric computation. After the stitch point set construction, the neighbor search algorithm determines the nearest neighboring points in the horizontal direction (left and right) for each stitch point to establish topological relationships along the stitch line. For instance, to find the right neighbor point qrn of point qi, the computation follows formulas (18) and (19):

[Normal] In summary, the neighbor search algorithm constructs a robust connectivity scheme by selecting the nearest points based on horizontal distance comparison, ensuring that the stitch point set accurately represents the spatial topology of the stitch pattern. Using only on as the detection criterion adequately meets requirements, thereby simplifying algorithm design and improving computational efficiency.

[Heading 1] 4 Experiments
[Heading 2] 4.1 Dataset
[Normal] The SPD was specifically constructed for the EPSPM framework. Utilizing common white T-shirt fabric as the background, the dataset covers eight distinct stitch colors, categorized into high-contrast (e.g., blue, brown) and low-contrast (e.g., pink, white, yellow) scenarios. All image acquisitions were performed under the customized illumination conditions described in Section 4.2 to ensure data consistency.
[Normal] Based on the requirements of the algorithm framework, SPD is divided into three specific task-oriented subsets (see Table 2):
[List Paragraph] Task 1 (Deblurring): This subset contains blurred-sharp image pairs generated for the eight stitch colors. To enhance the network's generalization capability across same-color and low-contrast scenarios, the training data for all colors were aligned and expanded to 250 training images per color.
[List Paragraph] Task 2 (Segmentation): This subset is used to train PSSNet for pixel-level stitch extraction. A heterogeneous data distribution strategy was adopted for this task. Experimental findings indicate that the segmentation network can learn universal stitch edge features from high-contrast samples and transfer them to low-contrast scenarios. Therefore, the training set for high-contrast stitches is set at 250 images per color, while that for low-contrast/same-color stitches is 100 images per color. This approach ensures algorithmic generalization while significantly reducing manual labeling costs.
[List Paragraph] Task 3 (Measurement): This subset is primarily utilized for end-to-end geometric accuracy validation. Unlike the previous two deep-learning-based tasks, Task 3 is a geometric validation task and does not have an independent training set. It consists of 26 high-precision images per stitch color for testing. Among these, 11 images are used for direct measurement validation, and the remaining 15 have undergone the full-process processing of the EPSPM framework to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework.
[Normal] It should be explicitly noted that the statistical values listed in Table 2 represent only the number of input images in each task. For Task 1 and Task 2, each input image is equipped with a corresponding pixel-level ground-truth image (a sharp image for deblurring and a binary mask for segmentation, respectively). In contrast, the "ground-truth" for Task 3 consists of high-precision physical measurement data. [Heading 3] 5.5.1 Sensitivity Analysis of Thresholds in the Image Blur Assessment Module
[Normal] In an automated quality inspection pipeline, the system must accurately evaluate whether captured stitch images are degraded by motion blur to determine whether the downstream ESDNet deblurring module should be triggered. Rather than relying on empirical heuristics, these parameters are determined through error rate scanning on the SPD validation set.
[Normal] Selecting the decision thresholds essentially involves finding the optimal trade-off between the False Positive Rate (FPR, i.e., misclassifying sharp images as blurred) and the False Negative Rate (FNR, i.e., misclassifying blurred images as sharp). Conversely, excessively stringent thresholds will misclassify low-contrast but sharp images—often caused by inherent fabric textures—as blurred. This not only increases unnecessary computational overhead but also risks introducing high-frequency artifacts due to over-restoration.

[Heading 3] 5.5.2 Performance Sensitivity Intervals of Loss Function Hyperparameters
[Normal] During the end-to-end optimization of the network, the weight allocation within the loss function directly dictates the convergence direction and final accuracy of multi-task learning. The proposed framework comprises two core stages—deblurring and segmentation—involving three key hyperparameters in total. To eschew empirical parameter settings, we designed multiple sets of hyperparameter tuning experiments. By evaluating the impact of different candidate values on core metrics using the SPD validation set, the optimal balance point for each weight was established.
[Normal] (1) Weight Hyperparameters in the Deblurring Stage (α and β)
[Normal] In the joint loss function of the deblurring module, the hyperparameter α controls the penalty magnitude of the Edge Loss, aiming to restore the edge details of minute stitches; meanwhile, β constrains the FFT Loss to ensure global consistency in the frequency domain. As shown in Table 8, we tested different magnitudes for these parameters. When α is excessively large, the network becomes overly sensitive to high-frequency Laplacian features, which tends to amplify sensor noise at the edges; conversely, a value too small results in blurred stitch edges and a loss of structural constraints. Similarly, the value of the frequency weight β requires a delicate balance between eliminating motion ghosting (which necessitates a sufficiently large value) and preventing ringing artifacts caused by over-penalizing high-frequency components (which restricts the value from being too large). Experimental results demonstrate that the combination of α = 0.05 and β = 0.01 achieves the best Pareto Optimality in terms of PSNR and visual perceptual quality.
[Normal] (2) Smoothing Hyperparameter in the Segmentation Stage (σ)
[Normal] In the segmentation module, to overcome the vulnerability of the traditional cross-entropy loss to label noise in complex, uniformly colored backgrounds, the model introduces an optimized loss based on Label Smoothing. The smoothing parameter σ is utilized to soften the ground-truth label distribution, preventing the model from becoming overconfident. Sensitivity analysis reveals that if σ is set too low (e.g., approaching 0), the model is prone to overfitting minor deviations in the annotation boundaries, leading to degraded generalization in low-contrast regions. Conversely, if σ is set too high, the model's predictions become overly smooth, causing extremely fine stitch features to be submerged by large areas of background pixels. The currently selected value of σ maximizes the IoU, perfectly preserving the intricate boundary details of the sewing stitches while significantly enhancing the model's robustness.

[Normal] Regarding the practical feasibility of the EPSPM framework in real-world production environments, this section conducts an in-depth discussion from two dimensions: hardware configuration and the trade-off between deployment cost and performance:
[Normal] Target Scenarios and Hardware Matching Logic: The proposed EPSPM framework is primarily designed for high-end automated sewing units integrated with SCARA robotic arms or six-axis industrial robots. In such highly automated systems, the core costs are comprised of the robotic bodies and precision motion control systems. In comparison, the computing hardware required by this framework (such as an RTX 4060-class PC or similar edge computing modules) typically accounts for less than 5% of the total investment in the entire automation unit. This minimal hardware investment yields high-precision closed-loop quality monitoring capabilities, providing an exceptionally high return on investment (ROI).
[Normal] Multi-scenario Deployment Comparison: To verify the framework's generalizability across industrial applications with varying budgets, we conducted performance validation of EPSPM on two typical hardware platforms. The results (see Table 9) demonstrate that even on a general-purpose industrial PC (IPC) with a lower configuration, the system can meet industrial acquisition and detection requirements by sacrificing a negligible amount of inference latency.
[Normal] Furthermore, our team is currently investigating low-cost migration techniques for the algorithm. By leveraging industry-standard TensorRT quantization technology, the algorithm is expected to maintain extremely low inference latency on cost-effective embedded edge devices (e.g., the NVIDIA Jetson series) without dedicated GPUs, while maintaining measurement accuracy. Future research will also integrate model pruning and knowledge distillation to further reduce the demand for hardware computing power, facilitating the adoption of this framework in a broader range of traditional sewing workshops.

[Heading 1] 6 Conclusion
[Normal] In the garment industry, sewing stitch detection faces challenges such as motion blur, low-contrast scenarios, and complex background noise, leading to insufficient recognition accuracy and low defect detection efficiency. Existing methods exhibit limitations in detecting rapid motion, same-color stitches, and fabric under stringent requirements. To address these issues, this study proposes a stitch pitch measurement-based framework for sewing stitch defect detection, with experiments conducted across its three core components. Based on these experimental results, the main conclusions can be summarized as follows:
[Normal] (1) A stitch pitch measurement-based detection framework, EPSPM, is proposed, along with the SPD dataset specifically designed for stitch pitch measurement. This framework addresses detection challenges in low-contrast scenes, motion blur, and complex backgrounds, achieving a single-image detection speed below 0.035 seconds.
[Normal] (2) To tackle motion blur during sewing, an efficient deblurring network, ESDNet, is developed. By designing the IncepFFT module to fuse frequency-domain and multi-scale feature extraction, ESDNet effectively restores clarity in blurred stitch images and enhances edge detail preservation, demonstrating strong PSNR and SSIM performance in the deblurring task.
[Normal] (3) To address the difficulty in identifying valid features of stitches and fabric in low-contrast scenarios, a lightweight segmentation network, PSSNet, is designed. Combined with customized lighting optimization, PSSNet achieves precise segmentation of stitches in low-contrast or same-color scenarios, improving performance in complex scenes while balancing accuracy and speed in the segmentation task.
[Normal] (4) For stitch defect detection needs, SPMM is developed. By measuring stitch pitch and employing pixel distance conversion, area judgment, and width constraint sub-modules, it achieves an average MAE of 0.126 mm, with errors for all eight stitch pitch types below 0.20 mm. Additionally, this module integrates three custom sub-modules to determine the presence of defects and classify defect types.
[Normal] These findings provide an efficient solution for automated quality inspection in the garment industry, demonstrating the potential of stitch pitch measurement in stitch defect detection. To further enhance its practicality, future work will focus on optimizing the algorithm to increase inference speed, expanding its applicability to various fabrics, and promoting its integration into intelligent factory systems.
